# Story: the sentence, the beats, the storyboard

A film is an argument that runs in time. Before any capture, write what it argues.

## 1. The sentence

One sentence, in the viewer's terms, of what they know at the end:

- Site film: "The programme is listed among the partner's schools; one click and you are on its page."
- Story film: "I joined a community as a member, and two years later I teach in it."
- Edit: "In twelve minutes, the three moves that fixed a sales pipeline, from the people who made them."

If the sentence needs "and also", there are two films. If the owner's brief is a list of things to show, the
sentence is the reason they matter; ask for it before building.

## 2. The six beats

Each beat has one job and a role. The role sets the hold floor the gates check (`review.md`).

| Beat | Job | Role | Site film, 15 s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | the brand in the first second; stop the scroll | glance | the host page's hero under a blur, both marks stacked over it, 0 to 2 s |
| Context | where we are | glance | the marks go to the corners, the page scrolls fast to the section, 2 to 5 s |
| Proof | the one fact, framed and read | key | zoom onto the item, a light on it, a second push onto its words, 5 to 8.4 s |
| Action | something happens because of it | (motion) | the cursor arrives, presses, the real click, 7.5 to 8.4 s |
| Destination | where the action lands | glance | the new page framed on its headline, then pulled back whole, 8.4 to 11 s |
| Sign-off | the bookend | end | the marks return in the hook's order on a framed card, the link, 11 to 14.8 s |

Rules that came from owners' notes:

- **Brand in the first second.** A brand that appears only after five seconds is a brand most viewers never see.
  One early cut showed the brand at 5 s and was sent back for a quicker start.
- **The host of the news leads.** On a partner's announcement the partner's mark comes first, in the hook and in the
  sign-off. The sign-off repeats the hook's order, so the film opens and closes on the same sentence.
- **Real evidence, real interaction.** The proof is the real page as it plays, never a redrawing; the action is a
  real click that really lands. The craft is in the camera, not in faking the page.
- **The destination gets its own camera.** Carrying the last page's zoom across a cut crops the new page badly
  (a review found the landing cut through a dark panel). Frame the destination on its own words.

## 3. Proof film or tour

| | Tour (30 s) | Proof film (15 s) |
|---|---|---|
| Argument | "here is the page" | "a partner lists the programme; come in" |
| Holds | many, short (0.27 to 0.4 s on the price and the partners) | few, at their floors (0.75 to 2.5 s) |
| Ending | an end card held 1.0 s | 2.5 s, the marks in the hook's order |
| What reviews found open at ship | holds, a faint click | none blocking |

A tour spends its time on breadth and starves every beat. A proof spends it on one fact and the move it causes.

## 4. The storyboard

- Render key frames at every beat's start, middle and end (`sv film DIR keys OUT t,t,...`) and lay them on one sheet.
- Look at them at phone width (390 px). Anything the beat says must read there.
- Show the owner the sheet before the first full render when the film is new; for a round of notes, show the film.
- Alternatives are stills of the same moment side by side (three to five, one recommended), never prose.

## 5. Three questions first

Ask these before any recon, in one message, each with a proposed answer the owner can take with a yes. On one long
film the owner's notes fell into these three groups, and most of them arrived as extra rounds of the film that one
answer up front would have saved:

1. **Who watches it, where, and whose brand leads?** The audience and the channel (the partner's own team by email, a
   LinkedIn feed, a talk), and the look that follows: whose marks, which ground, the page full bleed or in a browser
   window with its address. On that film four rounds went on the look (a white ground once the camera leaves the
   coloured hero, a bigger mark, a browser header with the address typed in on the cover, then that header through
   the whole film): one answer would have set all four.
2. **What must they remember, and what must the film never dwell on?** The sentence, and the list of things to pass
   quickly or never hold on: a competitor's card, a date that will be stale, a section the owner finds weak. On that
   film a late round was spent moving the zoomed-out views off a competitor's card they opened on.
3. **Where does the camera stop, and how does it end?** Three to five moments that get a dive or a glide, the rest
   a quick pass; the length; the last frame (the mark, the address, a click). On that film the first version the
   owner saw was a scroll with an even hold per section, and it came back asking for more zooming in and out, a
   choreography rather than a tour. Its answer is the choreography sheet and the camera map (SKILL.md, A1).

## 6. Then the rest, one at a time

1. The sentence (and so the one fact).
2. Whose news it is, which decides who leads, and whether the partner's page and mark may appear (a joint
   announcement, or their yes).
3. Every string on screen, character for character (the film adds at most a title and a foot line; every other word
   comes from the captured page). Words belong to the owner, or to whoever owns their copy.
4. The destination: the URL the click must land on.
5. The formats (1:1, 4:5, 9:16, 16:9) and the platform; the cover is a separate still.
6. For a story film: the footage the owner owns or may use, and whose voice tells it (the owner's own words,
   captions or none).

## 7. Length

As long as the beats need at their floors, and no longer. Add the floors of every beat, the hook and the sign-off
included, add the moves between them (0.5 to 0.8 s for a camera move, 0.3 to 0.45 s for a click, about 2 s for a
scroll), and that is the film. The worked example in `choreography.md` is 14.8 s because its beats sum to that.
